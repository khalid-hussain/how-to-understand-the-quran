
# 12. A Response to the Objections of the Orientalists

The Orientalists of Europe, whose scholarship has won widespread acclaim and
whose research has overawed many minds, at times adopt such crude and uninformed
approaches in their hostility towards Islam that even a person of only modest
knowledge cannot help but be astonished.

For example, the famous German Orientalist and scholar Theodor Nöldeke, whose
scholarly poverty is reflected in his claim, Allāh forbid, that the Noble Quran
is the composition of Prophet Muḥammad \hon{\PBUH}{\BODY}, wrote in his review
of the Quran for the Encyclopaedia Britannica:

> Muḥammad was familiar with Arabia, but whenever he refers to lands beyond
> Arabia, his ignorance becomes evident. For example, he attributes the
> fertility of Egypt, where rain is scarcely ever seen, to rainfall rather than
> to the inundation of the Nile.

The actual matter is as follows. When Prophet Yūsuf \hon{\S_HIM}{\BODY} was
imprisoned, the king of Egypt saw a dream in which seven fat cows were devoured
by seven lean cows, and seven green ears of grain were swallowed by seven dry
ones. Prophet Yūsuf \hon{\S_HIM}{\BODY} interpreted the king's dream, which the
Noble Quran records in these words:

\AYAH_AR_START
قَالَ تَزۡرَعُونَ سَبۡعَ سِنِينَ دَأَبٗا فَمَا حَصَدتُّمۡ فَذَرُوهُ فِي سُنۢبُلِهِۦٓ إِلَّا قَلِيلٗا مِّمَّا تَأۡكُلُونَ ٤٧ ثُمَّ يَأۡتِي مِنۢ بَعۡدِ ذَٰلِكَ سَبۡعٞ شِدَادٞ يَأۡكُلۡنَ مَا قَدَّمۡتُمۡ لَهُنَّ إِلَّا قَلِيلٗا مِّمَّا تُحۡصِنُونَ ٤٨ ثُمَّ يَأۡتِي مِنۢ بَعۡدِ ذَٰلِكَ عَامٞ فِيهِ يُغَاثُ ٱلنَّاسُ وَفِيهِ يَعۡصِرُونَ ٤٩
\AYAH_AR_END

\AYAH_START
He said, "You will cultivate for seven consecutive years. Whatever you harvest,
leave it in its ears, except for a little from which you eat. Then after that
will come seven difficult years, which will consume what you stored for them,
except a little which you preserve. Then after that will come a year in which
the people will receive relief, and in which they will press (fruit)." (Sūrah
Yūsuf, Q12:47--9)
\AYAH_END

In this interpretation appears the word \ITA{يُغَاث}, which is commonly
understood to mean that there will be rainfall. According to the German
Orientalist, mentioning rain in connection with Egypt is, Allāh forbid, proof of
the Quran's author's ignorance.

Reason is consumed with astonishment at such an extraordinary absurdity.

How astonishing that an uninformed person should presume to criticize the speech
of Allāh, the All-Knowing, the All-Aware. The following discussion exposes the
reality of this criticism.

[First]{.smallcaps}, the word \ITA{يُغَاث} does not necessarily mean "rain will
fall."

A group of exegetes maintain that it is not derived from \ITA{غيث} (rain), but
rather from \ITA{غوث}, meaning assistance, rescue, or relief. Thus, the meaning
is that after the famine there will come a year in which people will be granted
relief and their hardship will come to an end. Whether this relief comes through
rainfall or through the flooding of the Nile is not stated in the verse.

<!-- TODO ref Ruh al-Ma2aani -->

Imam al-Ālūsī \hon{\M_HIM}{\BODY} writes, "That is, they will receive rain, as
held by Ibn ʿAbbās \hon{\P_TWO}{\BODY}, Mujāhid \hon{\M_HIM}{\BODY}, and the
majority, in which case it is derived from _ghayth_. It has also been said that
it is derived from _ghawth_, meaning relief and deliverance. It is said, 'Allāh
granted us relief (_aghāthanā Allāh_),' when He removes hardship from us." (Rūḥ
al-Maʿānī)

<!-- TODO ref Anwar al-Tanzil -->
Imam al-Qāḍī al-Bayḍāwī \hon{\M_HIM}{\BODY} writes, "It may mean that rain will
fall, if derived from _ghayth_, or that they will be relieved from famine, if
derived from _ghawth_." (Anwār al-Tanzīl wa Asrār al-Taʾwīl)

<!-- TODO ref al-Jawāhir al-Ḥisān -->
Similarly, Imam al-Thaʿlabī \hon{\M_HIM}{\BODY} states, "It is permissible that
it be derived from _ghayth_, as held by Ibn ʿAbbās \hon{\P_TWO}{\BODY} and the
majority of the exegetes, meaning that rain will fall. It is also permissible
that it be derived from _ghawth_, meaning that Allāh will relieve them and
remove their hardship." (Al-Jawāhir al-Ḥisān)

[Second,]{.smallcaps} even if the view of the majority of the exegetes is
adopted, namely that the verse refers to rainfall, the Orientalist's claim that
Egypt receives no rain whatsoever is factually incorrect.

Rain may indeed be scarce in Egypt, but to deny its occurrence altogether is
contrary to reality.

<!-- TODO Kitab al-Buldan -->
Al-Yaʿqūbī states in _Kitāb al-Buldān_ that Egypt's coastal regions receive some
rainfall.

<!-- TODO ref al-Ḥaḍārah al-Miṣriyyah -->
_Al-Ḥaḍārah al-Miṣriyyah_ likewise states, "It is by no means correct to claim
that the Nile Valley is entirely devoid of rainfall."

<!-- TODO ref Geography of the World -->
Marsden and Oxford Smith's _Geography of the World_ states, "Rainfall here is
very slight, amounting annually to approximately one inch in Cairo and eighteen
inches in Alexandria, which lies on the Mediterranean coast."

[Third,]{.smallcaps} the regions in which the Pharaohs resided were, in any
case, areas where rainfall occurred.

It has already been established that Egypt's coastal regions and Cairo,
situated along the Nile, receive rainfall. Historical records show that the
Pharaohs generally resided at Memphis and ʿAyn Shams, both situated near Cairo.

<!-- TODO ref Taqwim al-Buldan -->
Abū al-Fidāʾ records in _Taqwīm al-Buldān_ that ʿAyn Shams was known as the City
of Pharaoh and was located only half a stage from Cairo.

<!-- TODO ref Muʿjam al-Buldān -->
Yāqūt al-Ḥamawī \hon{\M_HIM}{\BODY} (d.626 AH) writes in _Muʿjam al-Buldān_ that
Memphis was Pharaoh's city. Referring to its ancient remains, he states that
this was where Prophet Yūsuf \hon{\S_HIM}{\BODY} lived, where Pharaoh resided,
and where Pharaoh's temple at ʿAyn Shams was situated. Present-day Fusṭāṭ lies
between ʿAyn Shams and Memphis.

Originally, ʿAyn Shams was a temple visited by pilgrims, around which a
settlement gradually developed into a city. In reality, it formed part of the
greater Memphis region. (Muʿjam al-Buldān)

Imam al-Maqrīzī records in _al-Khiṭaṭ_ that Prophet Yūsuf \hon{\S_HIM}{\BODY}
settled Yaʿqūb \hon{\S_HIM}{\BODY} and the seventy-three members of his
household between Faramā and ʿAyn Shams, describing the land as highly fertile.

Even today, ʿAyn Shams survives as a suburb of Cairo. Muslims refer to it as
ʿAwn, while Europeans have called it Heliopolis. It is now a developed district
with large buildings and fine hotels.

<!-- TODO ref Safarnamah Misr -->
Qāḍī Walī Muḥammad writes in his _Safarnāmah Miṣr_ (1924), "It is said that this
place had the honor of being the residence of Mūsá \hon{\S_HIM}{\BODY}.
Pharaoh's palace was also located here, and it was here that Zulaykhā lived in
the residence of the Chief Minister of Egypt. There also stood a Temple of the
Sun, where sun worship was practiced."

These details establish that the Pharaohs lived near Cairo, where rainfall does
occur. Consequently, if Yūsuf \hon{\S_HIM}{\BODY} mentioned rainfall in his
interpretation of the king's dream, there is nothing factually incorrect about
it.

[Fourth,]{.smallcaps} the Orientalist attributes Egypt's fertility solely to the
Nile, but overlooks the fact that the Nile itself is sustained by rainfall.

<!-- TODO ref al-Buldan -->
Al-Yaʿqūbī states that Egypt and its villages depend upon the Nile, and that the
increase in the Nile's waters results from the rainfall occurring during the
summer months. (Kitāb al-Buldān)

_Al-Ḥaḍārah al-Miṣriyyah_ likewise observes, "It has now become entirely clear
that the Nile's annual flooding is the result of the rains that fall in Central
Africa during March, where the Nile originates. It is this water that flows into
Egypt, bringing with it fertility and vegetation."

<!-- TODO ref Tasir al-Manar -->
The eminent modern scholar Sayyid Rashīd Riḍā \hon{\M_HIM}{\BODY}, who spent
most of his life in Egypt, writes in his _Tafsīr al-Manār_ (2:65), "Egypt cannot
be regarded as independent of rainfall. It is often said that Egypt's life
depends not upon rain but upon the Nile. Yet the Nile itself owes its waters to
rainfall. The abundance or scarcity of the Nile is directly dependent upon the
rainfall in the regions from which it draws its waters."

In this connection, he cites the _āyah_,

\AYAH_AR_START
أَنزَلَ مِنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ مَآءٗ فَسَلَكَهُۥ يَنَٰبِيعَ فِي ٱلۡأَرۡضِ
\AYAH_AR_END

\AYAH_START
He sends down water from the sky, then causes it to enter the earth as springs.
(Sūrah al-Zumar, Q39:21)
\AYAH_END

He further explains that the tributaries and springs feeding the Nile are
themselves supplied by rainfall.

One should also bear in mind Pharaoh's own statement, recorded by the Noble
Quran:

\AYAH_AR_START
أَلَيۡسَ لِي مُلۡكُ مِصۡرَ وَهَٰذِهِ ٱلۡأَنۡهَٰرُ تَجۡرِي مِن تَحۡتِيٓۚ
\AYAH_AR_END

\AYAH_START
"Does not the kingdom of Egypt belong to me, and these rivers flowing beneath
me?" (Sūrah al-Zukhruf, Q43:51)
\AYAH_END

This connection is further clarified by another _āyah_ of the Noble Quran, which
also deserves careful consideration.

Among the signs of Allāh mentioned in the Quran is that He conveys water to
lands where rainfall is absent or so scant that it is insufficient to sustain
life. Allāh says,

\AYAH_AR_START
أَوَلَمۡ يَرَوۡاْ أَنَّا نَسُوقُ ٱلۡمَآءَ إِلَى ٱلۡأَرۡضِ ٱلۡجُرُزِ فَنُخۡرِجُ بِهِۦ زَرۡعٗا تَأۡكُلُ مِنۡهُ أَنۡعَٰمُهُمۡ وَأَنفُسُهُمۡۚ أَفَلَا يُبۡصِرُونَ ٢٧
\AYAH_AR_END

\AYAH_START
Have they not seen that We drive water to barren land, then thereby produce
crops from which their cattle and they themselves eat? Will they not then see?
(Sūrah al-Sajdah, Q32:27)
\AYAH_END

<!-- TODO ref Tabari -->
Imam Ibn Jarīr al-Ṭabarī \hon{\M_HIM}{\BODY} narrates from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAbbās
\hon{\P_TWO}{\BODY} regarding \ITA{الأرض الجرز}, "_Al-Jarz_ refers to land that
receives only such a small amount of rain that it is of no real benefit, except
for the water that reaches it through flood channels." (Jāmiʿ al-Bayān fī Taʾwīl
al-Quran)

<!-- TODO ref Ḥusn al-Muḥāḍarah -->
Al-Ḥāfiẓ al-Suyūṭī \hon{\M_HIM}{\BODY} writes, "According to one group of
scholars, _al-arḍ al-jarz_ refers to the land of Egypt." (Ḥusn al-Muḥāḍarah)

<!-- TODO Tafsir Ibn Kathīr -->
Likewise, Imam Ibn Kathīr \hon{\M_HIM}{\BODY} states in his Tafsīr, "The
exegetes commonly cite Egypt as an example of _al-arḍ al-jarz_, but this does not
mean that the verse refers exclusively to Egypt. Rather, Egypt is one example of
barren land. It is beyond dispute that Egypt possesses such land. Its condition
is such that if it received rainfall sufficient for its needs, its buildings
would collapse. Therefore, Allāh sends to it, instead of local rainfall, the
waters that fall in the lands of Abyssinia." (Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr)

<!-- TODO ref Al-Risālah al-ʿArshiyyah and in Minhāj al-Sunnah -->
The same explanation is given by Shaykh al-Islām Ibn Taymiyyah
\hon{\M_HIM}{\BODY} in _al-Risālah al-ʿArshiyyah_ and in _Minhāj al-Sunnah_.

In _Minhāj al-Sunnah_, he writes, "Barren land does not receive sufficient
rainfall for its needs, as is the case with Egypt. Ordinary rainfall would not
suffice for it, since its land is clay-like. If it were to receive heavy
rainfall, such as that which falls during the month of Ādhār (March), its
dwellings would be destroyed. Therefore, it is from the wisdom and mercy of the
Creator that He sends rain upon distant lands and then directs that water to
Egypt. This verse is evidence of the Creator's knowledge, His power, His will,
and His wisdom."

How remarkable that the very point which, according to Nöldeke, allegedly
demonstrates, Allāh forbid, the ignorance of the author of the Quran, is
regarded by Shaykh al-Islām Ibn Taymīyah \hon{\M_HIM}{\BODY} as proof of
Allāh's perfect knowledge, power, will, and wisdom.

It is also particularly worthy of attention that this famine was not confined to
Egypt alone. Its effects extended to distant regions. The arrival of the
brothers of Prophet Yūsuf \hon{\S_HIM}{\BODY} in Egypt to purchase grain is
itself mentioned in the Noble Quran, and the Torah explicitly records that they
came from Canaan to Egypt for this purpose.

Indeed, people from many lands besides Canaan travelled to Egypt in search of
grain.

records that during a flood in Yemen a grave was uncovered containing the body
of a woman.

This inscription demonstrates that the effects of the famine certainly extended
as far as Yemen.

The Torah, moreover, explicitly affirms that the famine was worldwide:

> ^53^The seven years of abundance in the land of Egypt came to an end.
> ^54^Then the seven years of famine began, just as Joseph had said. There was
> famine in all lands, but throughout the land of Egypt there was bread.
> ^55^When all the land of Egypt also began to feel the famine, the people cried
> to Pharaoh for bread. Pharaoh said to all the Egyptians, "Go to Joseph. Do
> whatever he tells you."
> ^56^The famine spread over the whole earth. Joseph opened all the storehouses
> and sold grain to the Egyptians, for the famine had become severe in Egypt.
> ^57^And all countries came to Egypt to Joseph to buy grain, because the famine
> was severe throughout the world. (Genesis 41)

These references establish that the effects of the famine extended well beyond
Egypt.

Even if one were to assume, for the sake of argument, that Egypt itself receives
no rainfall, the rest of the world certainly does. Furthermore, the Quranic
expression employs the word \ITA{النَّاس}: the people, not merely the people of
Egypt.

**A Further Reflection upon the Verse**

One should also reflect upon the wording of the verse itself. Instead of
criticising the Noble Quran, one ought to recognise its eloquence, miraculous
nature, and truthfulness.

The passage quoted above from the Torah has already shown that while famine
afflicted the entire world, Egypt enjoyed prosperity:

> There was famine in all lands, but throughout the land of Egypt there was
> bread. (Genesis 41)

This prosperity was, in reality, the result of Prophet Yūsuf's
\hon{\S_HIM}{\BODY} interpretation of the king's dream, in which he instructed:

\AYAH_AR_START
تَزۡرَعُونَ سَبۡعَ سِنِينَ دَأَبٗا فَمَا حَصَدتُّمۡ فَذَرُوهُ فِي سُنۢبُلِهِۦٓ إِلَّا قَلِيلٗا مِّمَّا تَأۡكُلُونَ ٤٧
\AYAH_AR_END

\AYAH_START
You will cultivate for seven consecutive years. Whatever you harvest, leave it
in its ears, except for a little from which you eat. (Sūrah Yūsuf, Q12:47)
\AYAH_END

As a result of this instruction, the people of Egypt were able to preserve
sufficient grain. The surrounding nations, having received no such warning, were
unable to make similar preparations.

Now consider the language of the verse carefully. Apart from \ITA{يُغَاث}
meaning "the people will receive relief," or "rain will come," and
\ITA{يَعْصِرُون} meaning "they will press", every verb in the passage is in
the second-person plural, directly addressing the Egyptians:

------------ ------------------
      تَزْرَعُون you will cultivate
       حَصَدتُّم you harvest
       تَأْكُلُون you eat
      تُحْصِنُون you preserve
------------ ------------------

These second-person verbs concern Egypt alone, whose people consequently
benefited from Prophet Yūsuf's \hon{\S_HIM}{\BODY} guidance.

Thereafter, however, the verbs \ITA{يُغَاث} and \ITA{يَعْصِرُون} shift into the
third-person plural. This rhetorical shift (_iltifāt_) is not without purpose.

It appears that because the relief and rainfall extended beyond Egypt to other
lands as well, the Quran employs the third-person form in order to broaden the
scope of the statement. Thus, no room remains for the objection imagined by
scholars such as Nöldeke, namely that Egypt itself is not a land characterized
by rainfall.

By contrast, the commands regarding cultivation and storage concern only the
Egyptians, and therefore remain in the direct second-person form.

And Allāh knows best what is correct.

[Note:]{.smallcaps} _Iltifāt_ is a recognised rhetorical device in the sciences of
Maʿānī and Bayān. It refers to a deliberate shift within a discourse from one
grammatical form or mode of address to another, such as changing from the second
person to the third person, from the past tense to the present, or similar
transitions. It constitutes one of the most refined features of Arabic
eloquence, and the Noble Quran contains countless examples of _iltifāt_, the
subtleties of which are well known to those versed in the science of rhetoric.
